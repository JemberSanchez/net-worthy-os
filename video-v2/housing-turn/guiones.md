# Guiones — crítico de guion (PROVISIONAL)

**Ganador:** `contraste`

> Same house. Same price. One buyer pays nine hundred fifty dollars more every month.
> Buyer one gets the twenty twenty-one rate: two point six five percent.
> Buyer two signs today at seven point three.
> On the median home, that's thirteen fifty a month versus twenty-three hundred.
> But here's the strange part. Inventory hit one point six million homes, and prices still rose.
> Buyer one won't sell and give up that rate. Buyer two can't pay up. So the market freezes.
> If you're buying now, homes sit thirty-three days, and that's room to negotiate.
> So which buyer would you rather be?

### `error`

> Waiting for home prices to crash? They just rose for the thirty-ninth month in a row.
> Most people think more listings means cheaper homes. Listings did jump, to one point six million.
> But the median price still hit four hundred seventeen thousand.
> The real reason: rates. Seven point three percent today, versus two point six five in twenty twenty-one.
> That's about nine hundred fifty dollars more a month on the same home.
> Owners locked into cheap loans don't sell. So supply stays tight, and prices hold.
> Here's what nobody tells you: homes now sit thirty-three days, so sellers negotiate.
> So are you still waiting for that crash, or negotiating?

### `misterio`

> Thirty-nine months in a row, US home prices went up. Even now.
> More homes are for sale than in years, and they sit thirty-three days.
> By every rule, prices should be falling. But they aren't. Why?
> The answer is the mortgage rate.
> In twenty twenty-one, it was two point six five percent. Today, seven point three.
> On the median home, that's about nine hundred fifty dollars more a month.
> Here's the trap. Owners with cheap loans won't sell low. Buyers can't pay high. Nobody moves.
> If you're buying, that standoff is your leverage: more listings, longer waits, room to negotiate.
> So how many more months can this last?
